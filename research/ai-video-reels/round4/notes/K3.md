## 1. The master model: how advertising actually works (for Claude's head)

1. **Most buyers are light buyers who barely think about you.** Ads work by **refreshing and building memory structures**, not by persuading in the moment (Sharp, *How Brands Grow*, 2010; Romaniuk & Sharp, *How Brands Grow Part 2*, 2016).
2. **Most of that processing is low-attention System 1** (Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, 2011; Robert Heath, *Seducing the Subconscious*, 2012). Feelings, fluency and familiarity drive choice. Arguments mostly don't.
3. **So the job of an ad is:** get noticed → be enjoyed (emotion) → be **correctly attributed** to the brand (distinctive assets + early branding) → link the brand to **buying situations** (category entry points) → leave a strong **end memory**.
4. **The short-term job (activation)** is legit too: offer, urgency, CTA. It decays fast. Balance both (Binet & Field).

Every principle below plugs into one of these steps.

## 2. Ehrenberg-Bass Institute — Mental Availability, CEPs, Distinctive Brand Assets

### 2.1 Mental availability & reach (Byron Sharp)

**Evidence:** *How Brands Grow* (Sharp 2010). Its empirical "laws" are Double Jeopardy (small brands have fewer buyers, and those buyers are slightly less loyal) and the dominance of light buyers in sales. Growth comes mainly from **penetration** (more buyers), not loyalty. Recommended talk: Sharp, "How Brands Grow With Marketing Science" (YouTube `G53MkkQ5dBo`). Wikipedia on Byron Sharp also summarizes the mental/physical availability framework.

**Real ad example:** **Compare the Meerkat** (VCCP for comparethemarket.com, UK, launched 5 Jan 2009). The character Aleksandr Orlov exploits the market/meerkat pun, so the brand name itself becomes the joke and the memory hook. comparethemarket.com went from the 16th to the 4th most visited UK insurance site, sales doubled, and market share rose 76% by 2010 (Wikipedia, "Compare the Meerkat"). **Mechanism:** the brand name is welded into the creative idea, so you can't recall the ad without recalling the brand.

**How to apply to an AI video ad:**
- Write for **light, non-category-engaged** viewers. They don't know you and don't care yet. Never assume prior knowledge.
- **Make the brand the hero of the idea, not a sign-off.** Test: if you swap in a competitor's logo, does the ad still work? If yes, it's under-branded.
- **Example concept (Boca Raton HVAC company "ColdFront Air"):** a recurring animated polar-bear technician named "Frost" appears in sweltering Florida scenes (melting flamingo lawn ornaments, a sweating alligator). Frost arrives and the temperature visibly drops. The character plus the brand name make one memory structure.
  - Prompt seed (Seedance/Kling): `Hyper-real Florida suburban street at noon, heat shimmer rising off asphalt, a pink plastic flamingo lawn ornament slowly drooping and melting, then a friendly polar bear in a navy "ColdFront Air" technician uniform steps into frame carrying a toolbox, frost crystals spreading across the driveway from his boots, 35mm, warm-to-cool color shift, comedic timing.`

### 2.2 Category Entry Points (CEPs) — Jenni Romaniuk

**Evidence:** Romaniuk, *Building Distinctive Brand Assets* (2018) and *Better Brand Health* (2022). Mental availability is measured as the brand's link to **CEPs**: the cues buyers have when they enter a category (who / when / where / why / with whom / with what / feeling). Brands with more CEP links (and stronger ones) get thought of in more buying situations. A related Ehrenberg-Bass/LinkedIn B2B Institute paper (Dawes, 2021, "the 95:5 rule") estimates that only around 5% of B2B buyers are in-market at any time. Advertising mostly builds memories for *future* purchase.

**Real ad examples:**
- **Snickers, "You're Not You When You're Hungry"** (BBDO NY, 2010–): owns the CEP "hunger making you not yourself, mid-afternoon slump". The celebrity transformations (Betty White in the football game) dramatize the entry point.
- **KitKat, "Have a break, have a KitKat"** (JWT, since 1957): owns the CEP "taking a break".

**How to apply to an AI video ad:**
- Before writing, list **7 CEPs** for the client (W-questions). Pick **1 CEP per ad**, and vary the CEPs across a campaign so the brand is linked to *many* situations.
- **Open on the situation, not the product.** The viewer's brain files the brand under that moment.
- **Example (Boca Raton med spa):** CEPs = "a week before a wedding", "after seeing yourself on a Zoom call", "reunion invite arrives", "post-summer sun damage". Ad #1 opens on a phone screen showing a high-school reunion invitation, and a woman freezes. Ad #2 opens on a frozen, unflattering Zoom thumbnail. The same brand character or sound closes both.
  - Prompt seed: `Close-up of a smartphone on a marble kitchen counter lighting up with a text: "20-year reunion — Boca Raton Resort, Oct 25!", a woman's hand pauses mid-coffee-pour, rack focus to her reflection in the dark window, morning golden light, cinematic, 9:16.`

### 2.3 Distinctive Brand Assets (DBAs)

**Evidence:** Romaniuk (2018). Assets are measured on **Fame** (what % of category buyers link the asset to the brand) and **Uniqueness** (whether competitors share it). Asset types include colour, logo, shape, character, celebrity, tagline, sound/jingle, font, pack, and ad style. Assets must be used **consistently and for years** to build. Changing them resets the memory.

**Real ad examples:**
- **Cadbury "Gorilla"** (Fallon London, 2007, director Juan Cabral). A 90-second spot of a gorilla drumming to Phil Collins' "In the Air Tonight", with no chocolate shown. The only brand cue is **Cadbury purple** plus the end line "A glass and a half full of joy". YouGov reported that brand perception noticeably improved, reversing the decline that followed a 2006 salmonella recall (Wikipedia, "Gorilla (advertisement)"). **Mechanism:** pure joy (emotion), plus a big purple DBA, plus an end peak.
- **McDonald's "Raise Your Arches"** (Leo Burnett London, 2022). The raised eyebrows mimic the arches, so the asset is strong enough to work with **no logo**.
- **Heinz "Draw Ketchup"** (Rethink, 2021). People asked to draw ketchup drew a Heinz bottle, which proves the shape is a DBA.

**How to apply to an AI video ad:**
- For every client, build a **DBA kit** before the first video: 1 colour (exact hex), 1 recurring character or mascot, 1 sonic logo (2–4 notes), 1 visual device (for example a transition or a framing), and 1 line. **Lock these into a reusable prompt "brand block"** that gets pasted into every generation, and use character reference images (Kling/Higgsfield) for consistency.
- **AI-specific risk:** generative models drift (colours shift, the mascot changes faces). Consistency *is* the asset, so use image references, a fixed seed or style reference, and grade the final to the hex colour.
- Brand block template: `Brand palette: dominant #00A3E0 cyan with white; recurring character "Frost" (white polar bear, navy uniform, round glasses); every scene contains a cyan object; final frame: Frost winks, 3-note ascending chime.`

## 3. Binet & Field — Long vs Short, Emotional vs Rational

**Evidence:** Les Binet & Peter Field, *The Long and the Short of It* (IPA, 2013), and *Marketing in the Era of Accountability* (IPA, 2007), based on the IPA Effectiveness Databank of hundreds of award-entered campaigns.
- Brand building (long-term, emotional, broad reach) and sales activation (short-term, rational/offer, targeted) work differently. Activation creates short sales spikes that decay. Brand building creates a slowly rising baseline and reduces price sensitivity.
- The often-quoted optimum is about **60% brand / 40% activation** for B2C. Later work suggests B2B is closer to 46/54 (*The 5 Principles of Growth in B2B Marketing*, 2019).
- **Emotional campaigns** are more likely to report very large business effects than rational ones. The 2007 analysis is often summarized as purely emotional campaigns reporting very large profit gains about twice as often as rational ones (around 31% vs 16%, reported).
- System1's "Cost of Dull" work (with Adam Morgan and Peter Field) adds emotional-response data: **dull (neutral-response) ads need significantly more media spend** to achieve the same market-share effect. Their claim is that dull creative is a hidden media tax.

**Real ad example:** **John Lewis Christmas ads** (adam&eveDDB, 2011–), for example "The Long Wait" (2011) and "Monty the Penguin" (2014). Pure emotional storytelling with minimal product, repeated annually as a **ritual**, frequently cited in IPA Effectiveness papers for long-term ROI.

**How to apply to an AI video ad:**
- **Decide the job first:** is this a *brand* film (emotion, story, assets, no hard sell) or an *activation* asset (offer, proof, CTA)? For a local business, deliver a **set**: 1 hero emotional film (30–60 s) plus 3–5 activation cutdowns (6–15 s) that reuse the hero's assets, music and character. The hero film primes and the cutdowns harvest.
- **Never let the activation version strip out the emotion and the assets.** Keep the character and the sound in the 6-second cut.
- **Example (Boca Raton restaurant):** Hero film: "Three generations at one table". Grandpa teaches a grandkid to crack stone crab, the camera stays on the laughter, and the restaurant's teal awning and wind-chime sonic logo appear. Activation: 8 s of the same grandkid cracking a claw, a "Stone crab season opens Oct 15" super, the chime, and "Reserve now".

## 4. System1 — Emotion, Star Ratings, Fluent Devices, Right-Brain Advertising

**Evidence:**
- System1's **Test Your Ad** measures the emotional response of real viewers (FaceTrace, second by second) and produces a **Star rating (1–5, long-term brand-building potential)**, a **Spike** score (short-term sales potential) and a **Fluency** score (brand recognition). Their social metric "**Fast Fluency**" measures brand recognition after **2 seconds** of exposure (system1group.com/test-your-ad, fetched). The database has more than 5M emotional responses and more than 120k ads. Their core claim is "entertain for commercial gain": **more positive emotion leads to more growth**, and **neutrality is the enemy**.
- **Orlando Wood, *Lemon* (IPA 2019) and *Look Out* (IPA 2021)**, drawing on Iain McGilchrist's hemisphere theory:
  - **Right-brain features** correlate with long-term effectiveness: **characters with agency, dialogue between people, a sense of place, a scene unfolding over time, implicit/understated humor, music with melody, a clear, living narrative, and "betweenness"** (people interacting).
  - **Left-brain features** correlate with weaker long-term effect: **abstraction or isolated products floating in space, monologue-to-camera, flat or rhythmic beats without melody, words on screen, close-ups of disembodied body parts, fast cutting, and hard-sell voice-over**.
  - Wood argues that advertising has drifted left-brain since about 2006, with a corresponding drop in effectiveness.
- **Fluent devices:** a recurring character or scenario that acts as a *vehicle* for the brand across many ads (for example the meerkats, **Aldi's Kevin the Carrot** in the UK, or GEICO's Gecko in the US). K5 notes reported System1 data that fluent devices and recurring characters are linked to higher profit gains (around 30% more likely, reported), yet are used in only a small minority of ads.

**Real ad examples:**
- **Aldi UK "Kevin the Carrot"** (McCann Manchester, 2016–): recurring Christmas character, a high System1 scorer, now a merchandise empire.
- **GEICO Gecko** (The Martin Agency, 1999–): a 25-year fluent device that lets any message, offer or price ride on a familiar, liked vehicle.

**How to apply to an AI video ad:**
- **The right-brain prompt rule:** default every AI ad concept to **≥2 characters interacting in a real place, with a small unfolding story, melodic music and implicit humor**. Avoid the AI-ad clichés: product spinning in a void, a model staring into the lens, kinetic text over a trap beat. AI makes the "floating product in abstract space" shot trivially easy, which is exactly why it is everywhere and why it scores low.
- **Invent a fluent device per client on day one:** a character plus a repeatable situation template ("Frost arrives at a new overheating disaster each week"). AI is uniquely good at this. Character consistency tools (reference images, Kling elements, Higgsfield Soul/Genjutsu IDs) make a mascot series cheap for local SMBs, who historically could never afford a Kevin the Carrot.
- **Example prompt (right-brain version of a dentist ad):** `Two elderly Boca Raton neighbors on adjacent sunny driveways, one polishing a vintage red convertible, the other watering bougainvillea; they exchange competitive glances, then both flash huge gleaming smiles at each other in slow escalation, sprinkler arcs catching light, warm 1970s film look, gentle ukulele melody, comedic deadpan.` End card: dentist brand character and sonic logo.

## 5. Kahneman — System 1 / System 2, Cognitive Ease, Processing Fluency

**Evidence:** Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011). System 1 is automatic and effortless; System 2 is slow, effortful and lazy. **Cognitive ease** (familiarity, clarity, repetition, rhyme, legible fonts) feels like *truth and liking*. Related findings:
- the **mere-exposure effect** (Zajonc 1968): repeated exposure increases liking, even without conscious recognition (Wikipedia, "Mere-exposure effect");
- the **rhyme-as-reason effect** (McGlone & Tofighbakhsh, 2000): rhyming aphorisms are judged more accurate;
- **WYSIATI** ("what you see is all there is"): people judge on what is shown, not on what is missing.

**Real ad example:** **"Have a break, have a KitKat"** and **"Beanz Meanz Heinz"** (Young & Rubicam, 1967). Rhythmic, rhyming, effortless lines, repeated for decades, which is fluency plus mere exposure. The US equivalent is "Nationwide is on your side" (a sung jingle).

**How to apply to an AI video ad:**
- **One idea per ad.** If the viewer has to *think* (System 2) to get it, the scroll wins. Use a single-minded proposition, a single visual metaphor and a single CTA.
- **Write lines that rhyme or have rhythm**, and repeat the *same* line across all cutdowns.
- **Show, don't tell.** Use one strong visual demonstration rather than 3 text claims. AI video makes impossible demonstrations cheap (see Volvo "Epic Split" in §9).
- **Familiar format + one twist** (Wood's "familiar-but-new"; Raymond Loewy's MAYA, "Most Advanced Yet Acceptable"). Use a recognizable genre (nature documentary, heist film, cooking show) and put the brand inside it.

## 6. Cialdini — Principles of Influence

**Evidence:** Robert Cialdini, *Influence* (1984; rev. 2021) gives the principles **Reciprocity, Commitment/Consistency, Social Proof, Liking, Authority, Scarcity**, and *Pre-Suasion* (2016) adds **Unity**. One key field study is Goldstein, Cialdini & Griskevicius (2008, *J. Consumer Research*). Hotel towel-reuse rose when the card said most guests reused towels (descriptive norm), and rose further with "most guests **who stayed in this room**" (local norm), compared with an environmental appeal.

| Principle | Real example | AI video application |
|---|---|---|
| **Social proof** | Share a Coke (Ogilvy Sydney, 2011): names on bottles drove sharing and UGC; US sales +2%, reversing 10+ years of decline (Wikipedia, "Share a Coke") | Show **crowds of real-looking locals** using the product; make the norm *local* ("Boca's most-booked…"). Use real reviews as on-screen text in a *scene* (a review read aloud by a character), not as a floating graphic. Avoid fake testimonials (see Legal/Ethics). |
| **Authority** | Every "dentists recommend" ad; Volvo Trucks using Van Damme as a stunt demonstration | Show the expert *doing* the work in a credible place (hands, tools, environment). Avoid an AI "doctor in a lab coat" claiming things, which is an FTC risk. |
| **Liking** | GEICO Gecko, Old Spice Man | A likeable character and humor make the brand liked, and liked brands are chosen. |
| **Reciprocity** | Free samples; Dollar Shave Club's free-value comedy | Give value *in the ad*: a tip, a laugh, a beautiful moment. The ad as gift. |
| **Scarcity** | Worchel, Lee & Adewole (1975) cookie-jar study: identical cookies rated more desirable from a jar of 2 than a jar of 10, especially when supply had just dropped | Show the last items/tables/slots *visually* (an emptying shelf, a booking calendar filling). Use it only if it's true. |
| **Commitment** | "Try it for 7 days" small steps | CTA as a small first step ("Book a free 10-min smile check"). |
| **Unity** ("we are the same") | Local pride campaigns; Apple's "Think Different" tribe | **Boca/South Florida tribal codes**: the I-95 commute, hurricane prep, snowbirds, the Mizner Park weekend, a 4pm early-bird dinner. "Made by your neighbors." |

**Pratfall effect (Aronson 1966):** competent people become *more* likable after a small blunder. In ads, **admitting a flaw builds credibility**: Avis "We Try Harder" (DDB, 1962, "We're only No. 2"), VW "Lemon" (DDB, 1960), and **KFC "FCK"** (Mother London, 2018) during the UK chicken shortage, which rearranged its own logo as an apology.

## 7. Rory Sutherland — Alchemy, Framing, Anchoring, Costly Signaling

**Evidence:** Sutherland, *Alchemy: The Surprising Power of Ideas That Don't Make Sense* (2019). Talk: "Alchemy" (YouTube `lhlS-Wds02M`, ~880k views) and "The psychology of digital marketing" (`hhQRH49Y54k`).
- "The opposite of a good idea can be another good idea."
- **Perception is reality for the buyer:** change how something *feels* and you change its value. His example is the Eurostar journey speed-up spending billions to cut travel time, when making the journey more enjoyable (the "put supermodels in the carriages" quip) would cost far less.
- **Red Bull:** a small can, high price and a bad taste are signals of potency. Weirdness is a feature.
- **Costly signaling:** *the expense of advertising is itself the message* ("this company is betting on itself"). This connects to Zahavi's handicap principle; the peacock's tail is an honest signal because it is costly (Wikipedia, "Costly signaling theory in evolutionary psychology"). Ads that look expensive or effortful signal confidence and permanence.
- **Psycho-logic vs logic:** "Don't design for the average; design for the irrational."

Behavioral economics tools:

| Tool | Evidence | Real example | AI ad application |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Framing** | Levin & Gaeth (1988): beef labeled "75% lean" is rated better than "25% fat" | "Under $1 a day" subscription framing | Frame the price per use or per day; frame outcomes as gains ("Get your Saturdays back") vs losses ("Stop losing Saturdays to your lawn"). Loss frames are often stronger (**loss aversion**, Kahneman & Tversky 1979), but use them sparingly in brand films. |
| **Anchoring** | Tversky & Kahneman (1974): an arbitrary first number biases estimates | Williams-Sonoma bread maker: adding a pricier model made the original sell better (classic story); The Economist decoy subscription (Ariely, *Predictably Irrational*, 2008) | Show the expensive alternative first ("A new roof: $28,000. Our coating: $3,900"). Show a luxury reference visually (a yacht, a 5-star hotel) before revealing an accessible price. |
| **Decoy / compromise** | Huber, Payne & Puto (1982) asymmetric dominance | 3-tier menus | Offer ads with 3 packages and the middle one highlighted. |
| **Costly signal** | Zahavi; Sutherland | Volvo Trucks "Epic Split" (Forsman & Bodenfors, 2013): a real, risky stunt proved precision steering; Burger King "Moldy Whopper" (2020): 34 days of decay proved no preservatives, at the cost of making its hero product look disgusting | AI makes spectacle cheap, which *weakens* the costly-signal value of "looking expensive". **Restore costliness by other means:** real-world proof filmed for real (time-lapses, real customers, real owner on camera), or long serialized craft (a 12-episode mascot saga). Be careful: an obviously AI "expensive-looking" ad can read as *cheap* once audiences spot AI. **Blend AI spectacle with one undeniably real element.** |
| **Reframe the category** (alchemy) | — | **Liquid Death** (2019–): canned water sold as heavy-metal beer-style rebellion, so a "boring" product gains identity | For dull local categories (insurance, plumbing, accounting), invent a *ridiculous frame*: "the plumber as action hero", "the CPA as nature-documentary predator hunting deductions". |

**How to apply to an AI video ad (example concept, Boca Raton roofing):** "Hurricane season as a heist movie." The storm is the villain casing the neighborhood, with a crew of rain droplets in tiny masks. The roofer is the security expert who's already sealed every entry. Anchor: "Insurance deductible: $10,000+. Inspection: free."

## 8. Attention Science — First 1–3 Seconds, Pattern Interrupts, Curiosity Gaps

**Evidence:**
- **Facebook IQ (2016):** people can recall mobile-feed content after only **0.25 s** of exposure. Facebook IQ (2015): **47% of a video campaign's value is delivered in the first 3 s, and 74% in the first 10 s** (reported). The implication is that the **brand must be in the first seconds**, because most viewers never reach the end.
- **System1 Fast Fluency:** brand recognition measured at **2 s** (fetched, system1group.com/test-your-ad).
- **Google ABCD** (YouTube): **A**ttract (immersive, tight framing, fast pace at the start), **B**rand (in the first 5 s, early and often, audio + visual), **C**onnect (emotion, humor, people), **D**irect (clear CTA). A Google/Kantar analysis reported ABCD-aligned ads gave about a 30% lift in short-term sales likelihood (reported, 2022–23).
- **Karen Nelson-Field**, *The Attention Economy and How Media Works* (2020) and Amplified Intelligence: **active attention**, not viewability, predicts outcomes. Attention per second on social feeds is short and decays fast. Amplified models frame-by-frame impact of **faces, objects, motion and text** (amplifiedintelligence.com.au, fetched).
- **Orienting response** (Sokolov): novelty, sudden motion, loud or unexpected sound and looming objects capture involuntary attention. **Von Restorff isolation effect** (1933): the item that differs from its surroundings is remembered (Wikipedia, fetched).
- **Information-gap theory of curiosity** (George Loewenstein, 1994): curiosity arises when people perceive a gap between what they know and what they want to know. It is strongest when the gap is *small and specific*.
- **Wood, *Look Out* (2021):** "broad-beam" (right hemisphere) attention is drawn by living things, faces, eyes ("the stare"), sounds and things that unfold. "Narrow-beam" (left hemisphere) attention is drawn by abstractions and text. Brand-building needs broad-beam attention.

**Real ad examples:**
- **Old Spice "The Man Your Man Could Smell Like"** (Wieden+Kennedy Portland, 2010, Isaiah Mustafa). From second 1: direct address ("Hello, ladies") plus an absurd continuous single-take transformation (bathroom → boat → horse). A continuous pattern interrupt. It won the Cannes Lions Film Grand Prix (2010) and passed 60M YouTube views (Wikipedia, fetched). Sales reportedly roughly doubled during the campaign window (reported by P&G/W+K; there was concurrent promotion).
- **Dumb Ways to Die** (McCann Melbourne, Metro Trains, 2012). Cute characters dying absurdly, with a catchy song, deliver a curiosity gap ("what is this for?") resolved at the end with the train-safety message. Over 350M YouTube views. Its 5 Grand Prix awards at Cannes Lions 2013 were the most for any campaign in festival history (Wikipedia, fetched).
- **Burger King "Moldy Whopper"** (2020): frame 1 is a visibly rotting Whopper, a pure pattern interrupt against the food-ad genre.

**How to apply to an AI video ad (the hook toolbox):**
1. **Impossible-but-real-looking image in frame 1.** This is AI's superpower. Use the *specific and familiar made strange* (a Publix shopping cart drifting on the open ocean; a flamingo wearing a hard hat). Novelty beats spectacle.
2. **A face or eyes looking at the camera, or a living creature in motion,** in the first 0.5 s (broad-beam attention).
3. **Sound-first hooks:** an unexpected sound in the first 0.3 s (record scratch, a single voice line, an animal sound). Assume sound-on for Reels/TikTok, but make the hook work silently too (captions are the safety net).
4. **Curiosity gap, small and specific:** "This 81-year-old just beat her grandson at…" or a visual mystery (why is there sand in the dentist's chair?). Resolve it *with the brand*.
5. **Start in the middle of the action** (in medias res). No logo intros or slow establishing shots.
6. **Brand inside the hook, not after it:** the brand colour or character *is* the interrupting object (Frost the polar bear in Florida heat). That satisfies both "attract" and "brand" in the first 2 seconds.
- **Example hook prompt (first shot, 2 s):** `Extreme wide aerial over turquoise Atlantic off Boca Raton inlet, a single bright red shopping cart floating alone on calm water, a seagull lands on its handle and looks straight into camera, crisp midday light, photoreal, 9:16, subtle dolly-in.`

## 9. Sound & Music Psychology

**Evidence:**
- **North, Hargreaves & McKendrick (1997, *Nature*):** in a UK supermarket, French wine outsold German wine on days when French music played, and vice versa, while shoppers were largely unaware of the influence. Music primes concepts (Wikipedia, "Sonic branding", fetched).
- **Sound logos** (audio mnemonics) are acoustic equivalents of visual logos, usually at the start or end. Examples: the T-Mobile jingle, the Intel "bong" (Walter Werzowa, 1994), McDonald's "ba da ba ba ba" (2003), and Netflix's "ta-dum". Sound logos are increasingly registered as trademarks (Wikipedia, fetched).
- **Wood (*Lemon*):** **music with melody** (right-brain) outperforms rhythm-only, beat-driven tracks for long-term effect. Songs and jingles are a top-performing DBA type in Ehrenberg-Bass research, yet they are underused.
- **Musical fit / congruence** (MacInnis & Park, 1991): music congruent with the message improves processing. **Earworms** (involuntary musical imagery) come from simple, repetitive, slightly surprising melodies.
- Sound reaches brand recognition **without sight**, which matters for second-screen and partial-attention viewing.

**Real ad examples:** "Dumb Ways to Die" (the song *is* the ad; it charted on iTunes); Cadbury "Gorilla" (borrowed emotional peak of the Phil Collins drum fill); Intel and McDonald's sonic logos; the US "Liberty Mutual, Liberty Mutual…" jingle.

**How to apply to an AI video ad:**
- **Give every client a 2–4 note sonic logo** (Suno/Udio/ElevenLabs Music, or a composer) and place it **at the start (≤2 s) and the end** of every ad. Plus one *brand theme* melody the cutdowns reuse.
- **Prefer melodic, human-feeling music over generic trap or "corporate upbeat" stock.** Consider a **custom jingle with the brand name sung**. AI music makes a local business jingle affordable, which is a huge, underpriced lever for SMBs.
- **Sound design the hook:** one distinctive foley sound in frame 1 (a crab claw crack, an AC hum stopping, a can opening).
- **Match the genre to the concept** (heist frame → spy jazz; nature-documentary frame → orchestral plus narrator).
- Example music prompt: `30-second warm Caribbean steel-drum and ukulele jingle, major key, singable 4-note hook on the words "Cold-Front Air", ends on the same 4-note motif, cheerful, 110 bpm.`

## 10. Colour & Visual Salience

**Evidence:**
- **Bottom-up salience** (Itti & Koch, 1998, saliency model): contrast in colour, luminance, orientation and **motion** captures attention pre-attentively. **Von Restorff:** the odd one out is remembered.
- **Colour as a DBA:** Cadbury purple, Tiffany Blue, T-Mobile magenta, Coca-Cola red, John Deere green/yellow. Colour is among the most commonly owned brand assets, but often *shared* within a category (low uniqueness), so ownership requires consistent use.
- **Faces and gaze cueing:** viewers follow the gaze of people in an image. Eye-tracking demos (a baby looking at the copy vs looking at the camera, James Breeze, 2009, widely circulated) show attention follows the model's line of sight.

**How to apply to an AI video ad:**
- **Colour-script every ad:** a neutral or desaturated world plus the **brand colour as the only saturated element** (the red cart on the blue ocean). Put the brand hex into the prompt *and* grade to it in post.
- **Isolation:** one brand-coloured object in every shot, consistently placed, so it acts as a visual sonic logo.
- **Use gaze to direct the eye:** character looks at the product or the brand mark at the key moment.
- **Motion contrast:** the one moving thing in a still frame (or the still thing in a busy frame) is what's seen.
- Avoid the default AI teal-and-orange "cinematic" palette for every client, because it erases distinctiveness. Choose a palette that *competitors in the category don't use*.

## 11. Humor & Surprise

**Evidence:**
- **Eisend (2009) meta-analysis, *J. of the Academy of Marketing Science*:** humor significantly increases attention, positive affect and attitude toward the ad and brand. It can slightly reduce source credibility, and its effect is weaker for high-involvement or high-risk categories.
- **System1 / Wood, *Look Out* (2021), "The serious case for humour":** humor in ads has declined, yet amused ads score higher on long-term effectiveness. **Implicit or understated humor** (character-based, situational) outperforms gag-and-shout.
- **System1 "The Startling Power of Surprise"** (Cannes Lions 2026 session with Peter Field, Karen Nelson-Field, Orlando Wood and Adam Morgan, fetched): surprise is a **multiplier of effectiveness**, used both *strategically* (unexpected brand behavior) and *executionally* (unexpected moments in the ad).
- **Incongruity-resolution theory of humor:** a surprise followed by sense-making creates pleasure. The resolution is where the brand must sit.

**Real ad examples:** Old Spice (absurdist escalation); **Dollar Shave Club "Our Blades Are F***ing Great"** (2012, founder Michael Dubin, ~$4,500 budget), which reportedly drew 12,000 orders in 48 hours (reported); **Snickers "Betty White"** (Super Bowl 2010); Dumb Ways to Die (dark-cute incongruity).

**How to apply to an AI video ad:**
- **Rule of three:** normal, normal, absurd. **Escalation:** each beat is bigger than the last. **Deadpan:** characters don't know they're funny.
- **Surprise must resolve *into* the brand benefit** (incongruity → resolution = the product truth). A random weird visual with no resolution is just noise and gets misattributed.
- AI is excellent at visual absurdity, so use it **in service of a product truth**. Example (Boca pest control): a gang of cockroaches in tiny sunglasses throwing a pool party in a Boca mansion; the doorbell rings; a single silhouette in the brand's uniform; they all freeze; the pool party is now empty except for a deflated flamingo float. Line: "Party's over."
- For high-involvement categories (medical, financial, legal), use **warm humor** (charm, smile) instead of ridicule, because of the credibility cost.

## 12. Memory Encoding — Peak-End, Branding Early & Often, Repetition

**Evidence:**
- **Peak–end rule** (Fredrickson & Kahneman, 1993; Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber & Redelmeier, 1993, "When More Pain Is Preferred to Less: Adding a Better End"). Subjects preferred to *repeat* a longer cold-water immersion that ended slightly warmer. The colonoscopy study (Redelmeier & Kahneman, 1996) found remembered discomfort tracked the worst and the final moments, not the duration (**duration neglect**) (Wikipedia, fetched).
- **Brand early and often** (Romaniuk; Ehrenberg-Bass): late branding means many viewers leave before the brand appears and the memory is misattributed. Many ads are wrongly attributed to the category leader.
- **Spacing effect** (Ebbinghaus): repetition spread over time beats massed repetition. This argues for **consistent campaign assets over many weeks**, not one viral spike.
- **Emotional arousal enhances memory consolidation** (McGaugh, 2004).

**Real ad examples:** Cadbury "Gorilla" peaks on the drum fill and ends on the purple brand line. **Apple "1984"** (Chiat/Day) peaks with the hammer throw, then ends on the brand reveal. **Liberty Mutual / GEICO** end every spot on the same sonic logo.

**How to apply to an AI video ad:**
- **Design the emotional curve explicitly:** hook (0–2 s) → build → **one peak** (the biggest emotional or visual moment, *with the brand present in it*) → **brand-rich end** (character + colour + sonic logo + line). Never end on a flat legal card or a fade-out. The end is half the memory.
- **Place the brand at the peak**, not just after it. The peak should *be* the product doing its thing (Frost's frost wave cooling the house).
- **Brand in the first 2 s** (asset, colour or sound; it doesn't have to be the logo), **mid-ad**, and **at the end**: at least 3 touches in a 15 s ad.
- **Duration neglect:** shorter is fine if the peak and the end are strong. A 15 s ad with a great peak can beat a 60 s ad with a muddy middle.

## 13. Ad-to-AI translation: the 10 meta-rules (distilled)

1. **Brand = the idea.** Weld the brand name or asset into the concept (meerkat/market).
2. **One CEP, one idea, one feeling per ad.** Vary CEPs across the campaign.
3. **Right-brain by default:** characters + place + interaction + melody + implicit humor. Ban void-floating products and lens-staring monologues unless there is a reason.
4. **Fluent device from day one:** a recurring character/situation series. AI makes this affordable for SMBs, so it is our competitive edge.
5. **First 2 s:** an interrupt that *is* the brand asset.
6. **Peak with the brand in it, end rich in brand assets** (sonic + colour + character + line).
7. **Emotion first, offer second.** Hero film plus asset-consistent cutdowns (≈60/40).
8. **Surprise must resolve into a product truth.**
9. **Restore costly signaling:** add one undeniably real element (owner, location, real customer, real time-lapse) to AI spectacle.
10. **Consistency is the asset:** lock the brand block (hex colour, character refs, sonic logo, line) and never drift.

## 14. PSYCH CHECKLIST (Claude runs this on EVERY ad concept and script)

Score each item **0 / 1 / 2** (absent / weak / strong); max 40. **Below 28 out of 40, rework before writing prompts. Any 0 on items 1, 2, 5 or 13 is a blocker.**

**A. Strategy (before writing)**
1. **Job defined:** brand-building (emotion, long-term) or activation (offer, short-term)? Is the ad built for that job? [Binet & Field]
2. **Category Entry Point:** which specific buying situation (who/when/where/why) does this ad link the brand to? Is it shown in the first seconds? [Romaniuk]
3. **Light-buyer test:** would someone who has never heard of the brand, and doesn't care about the category, get it in one viewing? [Sharp]

**B. Branding & memory**
4. **Swap test:** if a competitor's logo replaced ours, would the ad break? (It should.) Is the brand *in the idea*?
5. **Brand in the first 2 s** (asset, colour, character or sound) **+ at the peak + at the end**: at least 3 touches? [Fast Fluency, ABCD, Ehrenberg-Bass]
6. **Distinctive assets used, and used consistently** with the brand block: hex colour, character refs, sonic logo, line? Any drift from earlier ads? [Romaniuk]
7. **Fluent device:** does this ad use or seed a recurring character/situation that can carry future ads? [System1]

**C. Attention**
8. **Frame-1 interrupt:** is there something novel, alive, or familiar-made-strange in the first 0.5–1 s, without any logo intro or establishing shot? [orienting response, Von Restorff]
9. **Curiosity gap:** is there a small, specific question the viewer needs answered, and is it resolved *by the brand*? [Loewenstein]
10. **Salience:** is the brand colour the isolated saturated element? Do gaze and motion lead to the brand or product? [Itti & Koch]
11. **Works silent AND sound-on:** captions or visual storytelling for silent viewing, plus a sound hook for sound-on viewing.

**D. Emotion & right-brain**
12. **Feeling named:** which ONE emotion does the ad target (joy, amusement, warmth, pride, awe, relief)? Does the peak deliver it? Is the ad *not neutral*? [System1, Cost of Dull]
13. **Right-brain features ≥3:** characters with agency, dialogue or interaction, a real sense of place, an unfolding story, implicit humor, melodic music. **Left-brain flags ≤1:** floating product, monologue to camera, wall of on-screen text, disembodied close-ups, beat-only track, hard-sell voice-over. [Wood]
14. **Surprise/humor resolves into a product truth**, not random weirdness. Humor is calibrated to category risk (warm, not mocking, for medical/financial). [Eisend; incongruity-resolution]

**E. Persuasion & behavioral economics**
15. **One idea, effortless:** single proposition, a rhythmic or rhyming line, no System 2 effort needed. [Kahneman]
16. **Social proof / unity:** a local norm or local tribe signal (Boca/South Florida codes) where relevant. [Cialdini]
17. **Framing & anchoring** (for activation cuts): is the price or offer framed per day/use or as a gain? Is there an anchor shown first? Is scarcity used only if true? [Tversky & Kahneman; Worchel]
18. **Costly signal / realness:** is there at least one undeniably real element (owner, venue, customer, proof) to counter "cheap AI" perception? [Sutherland; Zahavi]
19. **Alchemy check:** have we tried at least one "illogical" reframe of the category (genre swap, villain, opposite idea) before settling? [Sutherland]

**F. Ending**
20. **Peak-end:** one clear emotional/visual peak with the brand inside it, and an end that is the most brand-rich moment (character + colour + sonic logo + line + clear, single CTA). No flat fade-out. [Kahneman; ABCD "Direct"]

**Scoring guidance:** full pass = 20 items × 2 = 40, threshold 28. If time-boxed, use the **core 15**: 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 18, 19, 20 (max 30, **pass ≥ 22**). Blockers are 1, 2, 5 and 13 at 0 in either mode.

**Integrity guardrails (non-negotiable, run with the checklist):** no fabricated testimonials, reviews, star ratings or "doctor recommended" claims (FTC Endorsement Guides, and the 2024 FTC rule banning fake reviews/testimonials, including AI-generated ones). Scarcity and "limited time" claims must be true. Disclose AI-generated people where platform or state rules require it. No imitation of a real person or competitor's assets.

## 15. Worked example: running the checklist

**Client:** "SunKissed Dental", Boca Raton. **Raw idea (before):** a smiling AI model in a white void, text "Best Teeth Whitening in Boca! 20% OFF", trap beat.
**Checklist hits:** no CEP (0), no brand in the idea (0), left-brain flags: void, lens stare, text wall, beat (0), neutral emotion (0), no realness (0). Blocked.

**Reworked concept: "The Reunion Photo"** (CEP: "an upcoming event where you'll be photographed")
- 0–1.5 s: a high-school yearbook photo comes to life; the 1996 version of a woman flashes a dazzling grin; a camera-flash *pop* sound. **SunKissed's signature sunflower-yellow** is the only saturated colour (her scrunchie).
- 1.5–8 s: present day, she's in a Boca kitchen staring at a "30-Year Reunion" invite, then rehearses smiles in the microwave reflection (implicit humor, a real place).
- 8–12 s (peak): reunion night at a recognizable Boca-style courtyard; the group photo flash; her grin is the brightest thing in the frame; former classmates do a double-take (social proof, surprise resolving into a whiter smile).
- 12–15 s (end): the real dentist (a filmed, real person, as costly signal) hands her a yellow sunflower; a 3-note "sunshine" sonic logo; the line "Smile like it's senior year." CTA: "Book a free shade check."
- Activation cut (6 s): the yearbook photo hook → the reunion flash → sonic logo + offer, with the anchor "Whitening at most Boca spas: $650+. Ours this month: $299" (only if true).

Prompt for the peak shot (Seedance/Kling):
`Night, warm string lights over a Mediterranean-style courtyard with palm trees, a group of fifty-somethings posing for a reunion photo, camera flash bursts, a woman in a sunflower-yellow dress at center laughs with a radiant natural smile, two classmates beside her do a surprised double-take, handheld 35mm, shallow depth of field, joyful, photoreal, 9:16.`

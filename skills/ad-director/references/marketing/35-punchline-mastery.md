## 0. The diagnosis in one paragraph

Our films are **all setup and no turn**. The camera drifts and the light changes, but nothing changes *in meaning*. A punchline is the moment the last 3–5 s **reframe** the first 3 s (19 §3f row 1). Beauty alone is a mood, and a mood carries no story. You need **an expectation, then a violation of it that the product explains**. The emotion that sells is the release when the violation resolves: laughter, relief, awe or "aww" [inf]. TED-Ed's comedy lesson gives the mechanism: audiences habituate to one emotion held too long, so **timing and contrast** create the response, and a well-placed turn "both relieves and creates tension" [TE].

**The ten rules**
1. **One film, one turn.** Exactly one reframing moment, landing at **60–75 % of runtime** (18–22 s of 30).
2. **The product causes, solves or *is* the turn.** If the twist works with any brand swapped in, it is a vampire twist. Kill it.
3. **Plant the turn in the first 2 s.** The hook frame must contain the thing the twist recontextualises: a prop, a sound or a line. Hooks are open loops; "when you do close the loop, open another one" [HD].
4. **Show the product used, not displayed.** Open, pour, sip, spray, light. The consumption moment is the emotional payoff (§3).
5. **Sound turns before picture.** A hiss, a click or silence is the cheapest twist cue, and it needs no cut.
6. **In a one-take, the camera is the editor.** Each reveal is a camera move: pull-back, tilt, orbit, rack, a pass behind a foreground object (§2.3).
7. **Pulse the brand; don't bookend it.** The pack is in frame by 1 s, glimpsed again at the turn, and heroed at the end. Brand pulsing cut ad-avoidance in eye-tracking work [TW]. Logo-in-context beats logo overlays (23 §1.3).
8. **Deadpan beats mugging.** Otto never reacts big. The world reacts and Otto doesn't. That gap is the joke (Old Spice, IKEA Lamp, DSC).
9. **The last line names the turn, not the product's features.** "Don't be scared. It's just water." [LD1] "This one's real." [HL] Max 6 words.
10. **Score every concept against the twist test:** *If I show only seconds 0–3 and 25–30, does the end change the meaning of the start?* No → rewrite.

## 1. Anatomy of a punchline in a short ad

```
HOOK        SETUP              EXPECTATION BUILT        TURN (twist/reveal)     PAYOFF = PRODUCT          BUTTON
0–2 s       2–10 s             10–18 s                  18–22 s                 22–27 s                   27–30 s
plant the   establish the      escalate (rule of 3) or  one violation, caused   product used on screen;   line + logo in context;
odd detail  "normal" & a want  hold a mood till it      by / solved by the      the sensory moment;       optional tag-joke that
                               habituates               product                 the reaction              re-opens the loop
```

- **Setup = a promise the viewer can predict.** Comedy and surprise need a confident prediction to break [inf, consistent with TE].
- **Turn = the one frame where the prediction fails.** Gilgamesh "walks into a bar" at the climax [TE]: the frame is the turn.
- **Payoff = the product as the reason the turn makes sense.** VW's "Force" is the remote starter [VW]. The kids' "beer" is just water [LD1]. Betty White becomes Mike after the bar [SN].
- **Button = a second, smaller laugh after the logo.** Hellmann's: "I'm competing with a condiment." [HL] Skittles "Romance" ends on a robber, a cop and a beaver catching Skittles [SK]. A button rewards a rewatch and so helps loop-heavy platforms [inf].

## 2. Fitting a full punchline into 30 s and into ONE continuous take

### 2.1 Beat timing for a 30 s one-take

| Beat | Time | % | Job | One-take note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Hook** | 0–2 s | 0–7 % | Odd detail + pack in frame (logo-in-context, 23 §1.3) + a sonic cue | The camera is already moving on frame 1 (16 §2.2) |
| **Setup** | 2–9 s | 7–30 % | Establish "normal" and the want | One clear subject; no new characters yet |
| **Build** | 9–18 s | 30–60 % | Escalate ×3, or hold the mood until it habituates | Each escalation **enters frame** as the camera moves |
| **Turn** | 18–22 s | 60–73 % | The single violation | It happens **on a camera move or a sound**; no cut needed |
| **Payoff** | 22–27 s | 73–90 % | Product used: hiss, pour, sip, spray, flame + reaction | Slow down (speed ramp is fine in one take) |
| **Button + brand** | 27–30 s | 90–100 % | Line, logo **on the pack**, optional tag-joke | End on a frame that can loop to frame 1 (19 P12) |

Rules of thumb [inf]:
- **Turn later than 22 s** leaves no room to show the product.
- **Turn earlier than 15 s** and the second half sags. If the turn must come early (performance cuts), add a **second, smaller turn** (the button).
- **Dialogue budget:** ≤12 spoken words in 30 s, VO or Vee only. Otto never lip-syncs (OTTO.md).
- **Brand pulsing** [TW]: pack visible at ~1 s, at the turn (~20 s) and at the end. Don't hide the product for 25 s and then dump a packshot. Cadbury "Gorilla" did that, but with a 100-year-old brand and 90 s [CG]. An unknown DTC brand can't [inf].

### 2.2 Production reality for AI one-takes
Generated clips run 10–15 s, so a 30 s "one-take" is 2–3 generations stitched at **hidden seams** (16 §1, §3.3). **Put the seams where the punchline structure wants motion anyway** [inf]:
- **Seam A (~10 s):** a foreground pass (pillar, person, door frame) or a whip.
- **Seam B (~20 s):** *the turn itself.* The turn is the moment of maximum change, so a seam there is invisible: a flash, a blackout, a splash or a freeze.
- Never seam during the payoff (pour/sip) or on the label.

## 3. Product interaction as the payoff

### 3.1 Why "used" beats "shown"
- **Embodied mental simulation.** Seeing a product in a way that invites interaction raised purchase intention across four studies (Elder & Krishna 2012, *JCR*; the manipulation was handle orientation) [EK]. **Caveat:** eight later experiments found no handle-orientation effect (Pecher & van Dantzig 2016) [PV]. The robust takeaway is the broader one: show the action, not the angle [inf].
- **"Last stage before consumption."** Showing a product at the moment just before it is consumed raised purchase intention, through mental simulation, **for hedonic products only** (Bai, Ma & Hu, *Current Psychology*, in press) [BM]. Candles, perfume, soda, chocolate and jewelry are hedonic. **The poured glass and the lit wick are our money frames** [inf].
- **How-to, recipe or unbox** ads delivered **1.5× behaviour change** (Ipsos, ~500 short-form ads; 23 §1.3).
- **Sound is half the product.** Coca-Cola recorded its drinking sounds binaurally because those sounds "create a feeling of satisfaction" (WPP case) [CC]. Its posters bet on viewers *hearing* the "psst" from a picture [CC]. Michelob built a Super Bowl spot on tap, pour and fizz [MU].

### 3.2 The sensory payoff kit (per niche) [inf]

| Product | The ritual | ASMR sound to record/design | The "first" moment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soda (AURUM) | ring-pull → pour → foam rise → sip | tab crack, *psst*, glug, fizz crackle, ice clink | **First sip**: eyes close; for Otto the moustache tips spring |
| Candle (SOL) | match strike → wick catch → first glow → wax pool | match rasp, flare *fwump*, soft crackle | **First flame**: the room's colour temperature flips warm |
| Perfume (VESPER) | cap off → two sprays → wrist to nose | cap click, atomiser *tss-tss*, inhale | **First breath in**: shoulders drop |
| Skincare | pump → drop → press into skin | dropper squeeze, pat | **First touch**: the skin catches light |
| Jewelry | box creak → clasp → in the light | hinge, clasp click, chime | **First wear**: a glance in the mirror |
| Food | tear → break → bite | crinkle, snap, crunch | **First bite**: the chew slows down |

### 3.3 The "first sip" reaction: rules [inf]
1. **Hold 1.5–2.5 s** on the reaction. Shorter doesn't register; longer goes cheesy.
2. **Understate it.** A breath out, eyes closing, a shoulder drop. Hellmann's works because the reaction is outsized *on purpose* as a callback [HL]. Without a callback, go small.
3. **Make the world react instead of the face** (AI faces are a risk, 22 §5): the light warms, the heat haze clears, the bubbles rise faster, a stranger turns.
4. **Sound leads by ~4 frames.** The fizz or flame sound starts before the visual peak.
5. **Logo legible at the moment of consumption.** The can label faces the lens during the sip.

## 4. Otto and Vee as protagonists without eating the product

**The risk is real.** In experiments with 4,970 people, a celebrity endorser *lowered* brand recall versus an unknown, equally attractive endorser (the "vampire effect"). High endorser–brand congruence mitigated it (Erfgen, Zenker & Sattler 2015) [VE]. Eye-tracking across 100+ brands: viewers looked longer at the celebrity's face than at other ad elements (Chan & Chau 2023) [VE]. Faces filling **<20 % of frame** gave **+31 % consideration** (TikTok, 23 §1.3). A fluent character gave +57 % brand lift (System1, 23 §1.3). A mascot helps only if it is *linked* to the brand.

**Rules**
1. **Strategic boundary (most important) [inf].** Otto and Vee are **VXO's** brand assets. In a client's paid ad, Otto advertises VXO, not the client, and the vampire effect works against the client. Use them in:
   - (a) **VXO spec films and VXO's own ads**: full protagonist;
   - (b) client films **only** as an opt-in "director's cameo" (≤3 s, Hitchcock-style), with written client approval;
   - (c) never as the face of a client's ongoing campaign.
   Offer clients their own character instead (30 §2).
2. **Otto is the straight man; the product is the comedian.** The product causes every surprise. Otto's job is to *not react*: deadpan, one raised brow (OTTO.md).
3. **The moustache is the reaction channel.** His mouth is never shown, so sips happen *behind* the glass or can, or the moustache tips spring, curl or droop. The weird feature "acts" (30 §2.2). This is also the safest AI shot: no lips, no teeth.
4. **Eye-line to product.** Otto looks at the product at the turn and during the payoff. Viewers follow gaze [inf: a widely shared eye-tracking demo; no primary source checked]. Otto never looks at the lens during the product moment.
5. **Frame share.** Otto's face fills <20 % of the frame [23 §1.3]. The product is the sharpest object in the payoff frame. The product's frame area during the payoff is ≥ Otto's.
6. **Screen-time budget.** Otto is in ≤60 % of the film; the product is visible ≥70 % (pulsed, §2.1).
7. **One gesture, one catchphrase.** Otto: lifts the brass viewfinder. Vee: clicks the stopwatch. Max one per film (30 rule 10).
8. **The product gets the last action.** The final frame is the product in use or in context, not Otto's face. If Otto is in the end frame, he is out of focus or a silhouette.
9. **Vee speaks; Otto doesn't.** If a line is needed, Vee says it (VO or on camera), or Otto's baritone VO is used over a shot where the moustache covers everything.
10. **The pair's comedy engine** (VEE.md) is *perfectionism vs. speed*. Use it only in VXO's own ads (it sells our service). In product spec films it distracts.

### AURUM — sparkling yuzu soda (gold can)

#### AURUM-1 "Dive in" (the founder's idea, developed)
Founder's brief: *Otto on top of something high above the whole city. He opens the can, pours it, drinks it, then jumps. Cars move below, in slow motion.*

**What's missing:** a jump is an action, not a punchline. The question every viewer asks mid-air is *"where does he land?"* The answer must be the product. **The twist: the city below was never a city. It is the glass.** The car headlights streaming in the streets were the **bubbles rising through the gold soda**. He dives into his own drink.

**Mechanism:** M1 scale reveal + M4 literalised "take the plunge" + T2 tilt reveal + M11 ritual payoff. **Emotion:** vertigo and exhilaration → delight. Release (not dread) is the target. **Payoff:** pour, sip, the moustache reaction, then the plunge *into* AURUM. **Line (Otto VO, dry baritone, 3 words):** "Don't sip. Dive." → super **AURUM**.

| t | Picture (one take: locked behind Otto → orbit → follow the fall → pull-back out of the glass) | Sound |
|---|---|---|

**Why it works:**
- The jump's suspense ("where does he land?") is resolved by the product.
- The first and last frames rhyme: a city of lights from above becomes a glass of bubbles from the side.
- Every beat of the founder's brief is kept: high above the city, open, pour, drink, jump, slow-motion cars.

**Alternative endings (A/B):**
- **(b) "Again."** Mid-fall Otto freezes, lifts the viewfinder, and the whole fall **rewinds** (M18) back to the ledge. The full glass is now empty. A dry beat. Vee's VO: "Take two needs a second can." Better for VXO's own channel.
- **(c) "Lighter than it looks."** He jumps and **floats up**, carried by the bubbles. Only fits if AURUM's claim is lightness or low sugar.

**Safety and platform note [inf]:** a man stepping off a building can read as self-harm. Mitigate all of the following:
- It is a joyful swan *dive* with arms wide, never a hesitant step.
- Slow motion and a surreal palette from the first frame of the fall.
- The physics turns impossible (bubbles) within ~3 s.
- No onlookers screaming; no realistic impact.
- Check Meta and TikTok ad policies before paid use. Keep a no-jump fallback cut: Otto is already miniature and dives off the rim of the glass in macro scale.

## 6. Concept QA checklist (all must be yes)
1. Do seconds 25–30 reframe seconds 0–3? (twist test)
2. Does the product cause or solve the turn? Would the twist break with a competitor's pack? (it should)
3. Is the turn at 18–22 s and on a camera move or a sound, not a cut?
4. Is the product **used** (open, pour, sip, spray, light) on screen with a designed sound?
5. Is the pack in frame by 1 s, at the turn, and at the end (pulsed)?
6. Is the line ≤6 words, and does it name the turn?
7. Are the seams placed at A ≈ 10 s and B = the turn, and never in the payoff?
8. Mascot: is it a VXO-owned film or an approved cameo? Is Otto's mouth never shown? Is his face <20 % of frame at the payoff?
9. Safety: no realistic self-harm, dangerous-act or underage-drinking reads. (Liquid Death drew "bad taste" criticism for its kids spot [LD1]. A soda for adults can't afford that.)
